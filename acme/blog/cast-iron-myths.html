Six cast iron myths that keep good cooks away

From the bench, by Dana Okafor

I started Acme in a garage, casting one pan and trying to make it right. In the years since, I've talked to thousands of cooks, and the same worries come up again and again. Almost none of them hold up. Cast iron has a reputation for being fussy and fragile that it frankly doesn't deserve — most of it is folklore passed down from a time when soaps were harsher and stoves were different. Let's clear the bench.

Myth 1: "Never use soap, it'll strip the seasoning"

This is the big one, and it's outdated. The rule comes from the era of lye soaps that really could eat through anything. Modern dish soap is mild. A little soap and hot water will not strip a healthy seasoning layer, because seasoning is polymerized oil bonded to the metal — it's tough stuff, not a fragile film sitting on top. Wash your pan like you mean it. What you should skip is the dishwasher and a long soak in the sink, because those invite rust, not because of the soap.

Myth 2: "It won't work on my induction cooktop"

Backwards, actually. Induction needs magnetic, ferrous cookware, and cast iron is about as ferrous as it gets — it's solid iron. An induction burner locks onto a cast iron pan instantly and heats it dead even, with none of the hot ring you get on a coil. Renters with glass induction tops tell us it's the best pan they own. One small habit: lift the pan on and off instead of dragging it, so you don't scratch the glass. That's the whole adjustment.

Myth 3: "It's too much maintenance"

The daily routine is under a minute: rinse, dry, wipe with a thin film of oil. That's it. Compare that to a nonstick pan, which asks for nothing — right up until the coating flakes and you throw the whole thing away. One of those is maintenance; the other is a countdown to the trash. I'd argue cast iron is the lower-maintenance choice when you measure it in years instead of minutes.

Myth 4: "If it rusts, it's ruined"

Rust is the fear I most want to kill. A rusted cast iron pan is not ruined — it's cosmetic, and it's reversible in ten minutes. Scrub the rust off with a chainmail scrubber down to clean gray metal, wash, dry completely, and re-season. You now have a perfect pan again. The reason we can offer free re-seasoning for life is that there's almost nothing you can do to a cast iron pan that we can't undo. Try permanently destroying one. You'll have a hard time.

Myth 5: "You need a whole set"

You need one good pan. Maybe two. We make exactly two cooking vessels — a 10-inch Skillet and a 5.5-quart Dutch Oven — because between them they cover searing, roasting, frying, braising, baking, and simmering. The cookware industry sells sets because sets sell; it has very little to do with how you actually cook. Buy the one pan you'll use every day, cook with it, and see what you're actually missing before you buy more. Usually the answer is nothing.

Myth 6: "Cast iron is old-fashioned and impractical"

It's the opposite of a fad, which people mistake for old-fashioned. A pan that goes from stovetop to oven to campfire, works on every heat source including the most modern one, holds heat better than anything with a coating, and can be handed to your kids — that's not impractical. That's the most practical object in the kitchen. It just doesn't get replaced often enough to keep advertising to you.

The point under all of it

Every one of these myths shares a root: they make cast iron sound delicate. It isn't. It's the least delicate thing you'll ever cook with. The reputation for fussiness is exactly backwards from the truth, and it keeps good cooks buying pans they'll throw out in a year. Buy one that lasts, learn the one-minute routine, and stop worrying.
