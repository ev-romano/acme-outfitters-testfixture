Seasoning 101

Seasoning is the one word that scares people off cast iron, so let's take the mystery out of it. Once you understand what it is, the whole "high-maintenance" reputation falls apart.

What seasoning actually is

Seasoning is not a coating that comes in a can and it's not a chemical nonstick layer. It's baked-on oil. When you heat a thin film of oil on iron past a certain point, the oil transforms — it polymerizes — into a hard, slick, plastic-like layer bonded to the metal. Do that a few times and the layers build into the smooth black surface people prize. Your food glides, the iron underneath is sealed against rust, and the whole thing gets better with age instead of worse.

That's the key difference from a nonstick pan: a coating wears out and flakes off, but seasoning rebuilds itself every time you cook. You can't wear it out. You can only neglect it, and even then it's fixable in an afternoon.

Your pan already has a head start

Every Acme pan ships hand-seasoned with flaxseed oil, so it's ready to cook the night it arrives. Think of that as the foundation. Everything you cook from there — especially anything fatty, like bacon or a seared steak — adds to it. The pan you use for a year will be noticeably slicker and blacker than the one that came out of the box.

How to build seasoning on purpose

You don't have to do this — cooking builds it naturally — but if you want to speed it up or reinforce a spot:

  1. Start with a clean, fully dry pan.
  2. Rub a very thin film of oil over every surface — the seasoning wax from the Care Kit, or flaxseed oil. Then wipe it back until it looks almost dry. This is the single most important rule: thin coats bond hard, thick coats stay sticky.
  3. Bake the pan upside down at 450–500°F for one hour.
  4. Turn the oven off and let the pan cool inside it.
  5. Repeat two or three times for a fresh pan or a bare-metal repair. Each round darkens and hardens the surface.

Fixing seasoning that's gone wrong

Dull or gray patches: the seasoning has thinned there. Wash, dry, do one thin oil-and-bake round.

Sticky or tacky surface: almost always too much oil. Scrub the tacky layer back with the chainmail scrubber, then re-season with a thinner coat.

Rust: the seasoning broke down and bare iron met water. Scrub the rust to clean metal, wash, dry completely, and season two or three rounds. Good as new.

A total mess: if a pan is truly beyond a quick fix, send it to us. We re-season pans free, for life — see Returns & Guarantee.

The one thing to remember

Thin oil, dry pan, some heat. That's seasoning. It's not a chore you have to fear — it's the reason a cast iron pan can last longer than the person who bought it.
