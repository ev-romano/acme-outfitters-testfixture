Cook on coals, not hope

The useful part of a cooking fire begins after the showy flames settle. Good coals give steady heat, fewer scorched handles, and enough attention left for supper.

Check before you strike a match

Always follow local fire restrictions, burn bans, campground rules, and weather guidance. If fires are not permitted, use an allowed camp stove or cook a no-flame meal. A piece of gear does not overrule a closure. Use an established fire ring where one exists, keep water and a shovel close, and never leave flame or coals unattended.

Build good coals

Start with dry, locally permitted fuel and a modest fire. Do not move firewood between regions. Add pieces gradually so air can move through the stack. Let the wood burn down until it forms a deep bed of glowing coals with little active flame. That bed is your burner.

A hand near the fire is a poor thermometer and an excellent way to lose arm hair. Read the coals instead: lively orange surfaces provide strong heat; coals under pale ash are calmer but still useful. Test the skillet with a drop of water or a small piece of food, and adjust before committing breakfast.

Make heat zones

Bank a thicker bed of coals under one side of the cooking area and a thinner layer under the other. The hot side sears and boils. The cooler side simmers, holds food, and gives you somewhere to move a pan when the onions get ambitious. Keep a coal-free edge for tools and safer handling.

Set and use the grate

Place the Folding Fire Grate on stable mineral soil inside the established ring. Open all four legs fully and make sure each bears weight before adding cookware. Its cooking surface sits 7 inches above the coals. Do not place it over loose stones that can shift, or across a ring where the legs cannot stand evenly.

Use Forged Ember Tongs to move one coal or small log at a time. Keep handles turned away from traffic, children, and wagging tails. Steel handles, kettle bails, and mug handles all get hot; use a dry mitt. Water turns a damp cloth into a steam problem.

Adjust patiently

Move cookware before dumping on more fuel. Slide a skillet between zones, rotate it if one edge cooks quickly, and give the metal time to respond. The Camp Skillet’s carbon steel heats faster than thick cast iron. A small change in coal placement often does more than a large new log.

Leave it better

When cooking is done, let the grate cool completely away from foot traffic. Extinguish the fire with water, stir the coals, add more water, and repeat until everything is cold to the touch. Do not bury live coals. Pack out food scraps, foil, and trash. Scatter nothing that was not already part of the site, and follow Leave No Trace guidance and local rules for ash.

Brush cold ash from the grate and store it in its canvas sleeve. Pack it in, pack it out, and leave the next cook a clean ring. For cleaning after the trip, see gear care.
