Reading your coals

A campfire has no numbered dial. It does have color, ash, sound, and time. Learn those signals and you can stop treating supper like a wager.

First, make sure a fire is allowed. Check current local fire restrictions, burn bans, land-manager rules, and weather. Use an existing fire ring when provided, keep water and a shovel close, and never leave fire or coals unattended. During a ban, cook on an allowed camp stove or eat something cold. No meal is worth ignoring a closure.

Flame is dramatic; coals are useful

High yellow flames throw uneven heat and lick around pan sides. They blacken handles, scorch one edge of dinner, and change whenever a log shifts. Flames are useful for building the coal bed. For most cooking, wait until the wood has broken down into glowing chunks with little active flame.

Start with dry, locally permitted wood. Do not transport firewood between regions. Build a modest fire with room for air, then feed it gradually. Large rounds make longer-lived coals; smaller pieces get you there sooner. The exact wood matters less than learning the fuel available at your site and allowing enough time. Starting breakfast after everyone is hungry is how raw potatoes meet burnt onions.

Look at color and ash

Fresh, lively coals show orange through their cracks and radiate strong heat. A pale gray coat of ash softens their output, though the center may still be hot. Dark coals with only a dull internal glow are fading. Stirring exposes hotter surfaces and briefly increases heat. Adding a small dry piece extends the bed but may bring flame back, so place new fuel away from delicate cookware.

At night, every coal looks fierce. In daylight, heat is easier to underestimate because the glow is less visible. Treat time and food response as evidence alongside color. Wind also drives combustion, making the windward side of a bed burn faster and pushing heat toward one part of the pan.

Listen to the skillet

Place The Camp Skillet over the grate and warm it gradually. Add oil, then a small test piece of food. A steady, conversational sizzle is useful for browning. Silence means the pan needs more heat or time. A violent crackle, smoking oil, and immediate black edges mean the pan should move to a cooler zone.

The food is a better instrument than your bare hand. Water should skitter and disappear, not explode into steam. Onions should sound active without coloring in seconds. Pancake edges should set before the bottom becomes dark. Learn one or two familiar foods and use their response to read an unfamiliar fire.

Build a dial out of space

Rake a thick bed of coals beneath one side of your cooking surface and a thin bed beneath the other. Now you have hot and warm zones. Keep a third edge free of coals if the fire ring allows; it becomes a safer parking place. Moving the pan is faster and more precise than trying to rebuild the fire every time the heat changes.

The Folding Fire Grate stands 7 inches above the coals on four opened legs. Set every leg on stable mineral soil inside the established ring before loading the grate. Do not balance it across loose rocks. Use Forged Ember Tongs to shift one coal or small log at a time, and keep the tips used on fuel away from food unless you clean them.

Account for the cookware

Two millimeters of carbon steel changes temperature faster than a heavy iron pan. That responsiveness is helpful, but it means a skillet can run from ready to too hot while you chop the last pepper. Stainless kettles tolerate direct heat well, though their bases gather cosmetic soot. Enamel mugs may be heated only when not empty. Enamel plates do not belong over flame.

Whatever the material, assume every metal handle is hot. Use a dry mitt. A wet towel can make steam against skin. Turn handles away from paths, children, and dogs, and establish one place for hot tools so nobody has to guess.

Manage the long cook

For a pot of beans or a slow braise, make a larger coal bed than the first few minutes require. Keep a small feeder fire or new fuel to the side only where rules and the fire ring safely permit it. Move mature coals under the pot as the main bed fades. Add them in small amounts and wait for the result.

For quick food, stop feeding the fire early. Residual coals often last through the meal and cleanup. A fire that is smaller than your pride is easier to cook over and much easier to extinguish.

Close the kitchen completely

When cooking is finished, move cold cookware and tools aside. Douse the fire with water, stir every coal and pocket of ash, add more water, and repeat. Everything must be cold to the touch before you leave. Do not bury heat, which can smolder and burn the next visitor. Follow the site’s rules for ash.

Pack out food scraps, foil, and trash. Brush the grate only after it is cool and slide it into its canvas sleeve when dry. Leave No Trace is not a slogan for the mug; it is the last job before the road home.
