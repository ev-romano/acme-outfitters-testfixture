One-skillet camp breakfast

Crisp potatoes. Soft eggs. Four fed people and one pan to wash. The trick is not a secret ingredient; it is giving each ingredient the heat and room it needs.

This breakfast serves four from The Camp Skillet. It works over a fire grate or camp stove. Before building any fire, check current local fire restrictions, burn bans, weather, and campground rules. Use an established fire ring, keep water close, and never leave heat unattended. If flame is not allowed, a permitted stove does the job cleanly.

What to pack

  • 1½ lb small potatoes
  • 1 small yellow onion
  • 2 tablespoons cooking oil, plus a little more if needed
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • Black pepper
  • 8 eggs
  • A handful of chopped herbs or scallions
  • Hot sauce, if your camp has opinions

You will also need a knife, board, spatula, bowl or mug, fork or small whisk, dry heat mitt, and a lid or plate wide enough to cover the skillet. The Waxed Canvas Utensil Roll carries most of the small tools, but bring a real cutting board. A cooler lid is not improved by onion scars.

Do the quiet work first

Cut the potatoes into pieces about the size of the end of your thumb. Consistent pieces finish together. Dice the onion. Crack the eggs into the bowl, season them with a pinch of salt, and beat until the whites and yolks are just combined. Keep the eggs cold until the potatoes are nearly ready.

If you want the fastest morning, boil the whole potatoes at home until a knife meets a little resistance, cool them, and pack them cold. At camp, cut them and proceed. Raw potatoes work too; they simply need more covered time and a splash of water.

Build two heat zones

For a fire, burn dry, locally permitted wood down to a sturdy coal bed. Bank more coals beneath one side of the grate and fewer beneath the other. The lively side browns potatoes. The gentle side cooks eggs without turning their bottoms into a doormat. On a camp stove, the same changes happen with the control knob and by briefly lifting the pan away.

Set the empty skillet over medium heat for a minute or two. Carbon steel responds quickly, so there is no prize for blasting it. Add the oil. When it moves easily across the surface, add one potato. A confident sizzle means go; silence means wait; furious spitting means shift to cooler heat.

Cook the potatoes

Add the potatoes in a mostly single layer and season with most of the salt. Leave them alone for several minutes. Browning happens during the part where you resist poking. Turn them, add the onion, and move the skillet between hot and medium zones as needed.

For raw potatoes, add a small splash of water and cover the skillet. Steam until a piece yields when pressed with the spatula, then uncover and let the water cook away. Return the pan to stronger heat to restore the crisp edges. For par-cooked potatoes, skip the water and cover only if the centers need a final nudge.

When the potatoes are brown and tender, taste one. Adjust salt and pepper now. Push the mixture around the edge, leaving an open center. Add a light film of oil to that bare steel if it looks dry.

Finish with the eggs

Move the skillet to the cooler zone. Pour the eggs into the center. Wait until the bottom just begins to set, then draw the spatula slowly through the eggs, folding soft curds toward the potatoes. Rotate the pan if one side is moving faster. When the eggs are still slightly glossy, fold the potatoes through and pull the skillet from the heat. Residual heat will finish the job.

Scatter herbs over the top and carry the skillet to a stable serving spot with a dry mitt. Turn the hot handle away from knees and curious hands. Serve at once. A warm tortilla underneath catches everything; toast works too if someone remembered it.

Make it yours

Add chopped cooked sausage with the onion, or fold in sturdy greens just before the eggs. Cheese goes on at the end. Very wet vegetables should be cooked separately or added sparingly, because a crowded skillet steams instead of browning. If feeding more than four, cook potatoes in batches rather than building a root-vegetable hill.

Clean up and leave it better

While the skillet is warm, scrape out food and wipe away oil. Rinse with hot water when practical, dry it completely over low heat, and apply a whisper-thin coat of cooking oil. The skillet is not dishwasher safe. Pack food scraps and trash out rather than feeding the fire or wildlife.

Extinguish the fire with water, stir, add more water, and repeat until every coal is cold to the touch. Follow local rules for ash and Leave No Trace. For a deeper look at managing the heat beneath breakfast, read Reading your coals. For pan cleanup and rust rescue, visit gear care.
