Why cast iron — an honest case

We sell cast iron, so of course we like it. But we'd rather talk you out of it than sell you the wrong pan, so here's the real picture — the good, the tradeoffs, and the myths.

Do you even need cast iron?

Honestly, maybe not — and that's fine. If you cook rarely, hate any upkeep, and only ever make scrambled eggs, a light nonstick pan is a reasonable tool and we won't pretend otherwise. Cast iron earns its place if you want a pan that sears well, lasts for decades, and can go from stovetop to oven to fire. If you're the kind of person who keeps their tools for twenty years, it's the obvious pick. If you're not, we'd rather you know that before you buy.

Cast iron vs nonstick

Nonstick is genuinely easier on day one: light, slick, forgiving. The catch is that the coating wears out. Most nonstick pans last a year or two before they scratch or the surface degrades, and then they're landfill. Cast iron is the opposite trade — a little more effort up front, and then it lasts essentially forever and gets more non-stick the more you use it. Over ten years, cast iron is cheaper, better for a sear, and still going. Over one omelet, nonstick wins. Pick based on how long you're playing.

The maintenance myth

The biggest reason people avoid cast iron is a fear that it's high-maintenance. It isn't, and the myth does real damage. The actual routine: cook, wipe it out, rinse under hot water, dry it, wipe on a thin film of oil. Thirty seconds. You do not need to avoid all soap — a little modern dish soap won't strip a well-seasoned pan. You don't soak it, and you don't scrub it raw. If something sticks, a chainmail scrubber lifts it in seconds. That's the whole burden. It's less than people expect and far less than the internet makes it sound.

The rust fear

Rust is the other big worry, and it's overblown. Iron rusts when it sits wet — a pan left in the sink, or run through a dishwasher. Dry it and give it a wipe of oil and it simply won't rust. And here's the part that matters: even if it does rust, the pan is not ruined. A minute with a chainmail scrubber and a re-season brings it right back to black. Rust on cast iron is a chore, not a death. Nothing you can do to one of our pans is permanent — which is exactly why we can guarantee it for life.

Induction and every other cooktop

Cast iron is magnetic, so it works on induction — plus gas, electric, ceramic, oven, broiler, and campfire. It is the single most universal cookware material there is. If you're moving to an induction range and worried your pans won't work, cast iron is the safe bet, not the risky one. Just start at a lower setting than you would on gas, because induction heats iron fast.

The no-coating angle

There's no synthetic non-stick coating on cast iron — nothing to scratch off into food, nothing to break down at high heat. The "non-stick" comes from seasoning, which is just baked-on oil. For people who'd rather not cook on a chemical coating, that's a real draw. We'll be careful here: we're not making medical claims, just pointing out that seasoned iron is oil and metal, full stop. As a bonus, a little iron leaches into acidic foods, which some cooks like.

Weight

Cast iron is heavy, and we won't wave that away. Our 10-inch skillet is about 4.5 lb; the Dutch oven is around 11 lb with its lid. That mass is what stores and holds heat for a good sear — it's a feature, not a flaw — but it's real weight. If you have wrist or grip trouble, use two hands and the helper hole, and start with the lighter skillet. It's the one honest tradeoff of the material, and only you can decide if it's worth it. For most cooks, it is.

So, is it for you?

If you want cookware you buy once and keep for life, that sears beautifully and works on any stove — yes. If you want featherweight and zero upkeep — no, and that's okay. When you're ready, the Shop has all four pieces, and our guarantee means the decision is low-risk either way.
